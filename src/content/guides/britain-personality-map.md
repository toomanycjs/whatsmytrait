London is the most open and outgoing part of Britain but one of the least agreeable, much of Scotland is the most agreeable and emotionally steady, and Wales scores lowest on emotional stability. That is the picture from the largest map of personality in Britain, built by Jason Rentfrow, Markus Jokela and Michael Lamb from 386,375 people who took the BBC's Big Personality Test (Rentfrow, Jokela and Lamb, 2015).

## How the study worked

Between November 2009 and April 2011, more than half a million people completed the BBC test online, a 44-statement Big Five questionnaire. The researchers kept 386,375 people in England, Scotland and Wales and placed each in one of 380 local authority areas using the first half of their postcode. Northern Ireland was left out because of small sample size.

The volunteers were 64% women, with an average age of 36, and about 45% held a degree. The authors checked each area's sample against the 2011 Census and found it tracked population size, age and ethnic make-up closely, so they describe the area samples as fairly representative.

## What it found, trait by trait

| Trait | Higher in | Lower in |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Openness | London boroughs, Oxford, Cambridge, Brighton, Bristol, Manchester, Glasgow, parts of Wales | Much of the East Midlands and East of England |
| Extraversion | London, parts of the South and South East, Yorkshire, Manchester, pockets of Scotland | East Midlands, Wales, Humberside, the North of England, East Scotland |
| Agreeableness | Most of Scotland, parts of the North, South West and East of England | London and much of the East of England |
| Conscientiousness | Much of southern England, pockets of the Midlands, the Scottish Highlands | London, Wales, parts of the North |
| Neuroticism | Most of Wales, parts of the Midlands | The South West, much of southern England, most of Scotland |

Neighbouring areas tended to resemble each other, so the traits form broad regional patches rather than a random scatter.

## The extremes

The University of Cambridge summary lists the highest and lowest scoring areas for each trait, as percentile ranks among all areas: Hackney topped openness, Hammersmith and Fulham extraversion, and Orkney had the lowest neuroticism. Barrow-in-Furness had the highest neuroticism and Merthyr Tydfil the lowest conscientiousness (University of Cambridge, 2015).

Treat single-area extremes with care. The Isles of Scilly topped agreeableness and conscientiousness from just 28 people, and the City of London, lowest on agreeableness, has a very small resident population.

## Why places might differ

The authors offer three explanations and say plainly that none has been tested here. People may move to places that suit them: a city draws the curious and sociable. Places may shape the people who live there through local norms and social life. And physical surroundings, from population density to landscape, may play a part. The study had no data following people over time, so it cannot say which matters most.

## What regional scores line up with

Area averages were linked to the area's education, income, occupations, health and life expectancy, using Census, ONS and NHS figures. Areas higher in neuroticism, for example, tended to have more long-term health problems and shorter life expectancy, and those links held after allowing for income, age and sex. These are links between area averages, not between individual people, and they do not show which causes which.

## What it does not mean

An area average says nothing about any one person. Every district has people at both ends of every trait, and where you live does not set your personality. The percentile ranks also compare areas with each other, not people: an area at the 90th percentile for openness has an average a little higher than most areas, not residents who are all highly open.
