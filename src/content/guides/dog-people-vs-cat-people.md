Yes, a modest one. In a study of 4,565 people led by Samuel Gosling, those who called themselves dog people scored higher on extraversion, agreeableness and conscientiousness, while self-described cat people scored higher on openness and neuroticism. The two groups still overlap far more than they differ.

## How the study worked

In April 2009, Gosling, Carson Sandy and Jeff Potter added one question to a free personality website that people found through search engines and word of mouth: are you a dog person, a cat person, both, or neither? Because the site was not about pets, the people answering were not a group of keen pet owners.

Of the 4,565 who answered, 46% said dog person, 12% cat person, 28% both and 15% neither. Everyone also completed a 44-item Big Five questionnaire. The sample was young, with an average age of 23, two thirds from the US, and 63% women.

## What it found

| Trait | Dog people compared with cat people | Size of difference (d) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Extraversion | Higher | 0.40 |
| Agreeableness | Higher | 0.33 |
| Neuroticism | Lower | 0.30 |
| Conscientiousness | Higher | 0.27 |
| Openness | Lower | 0.27 |

The differences held when the researchers allowed for sex, and when they looked at men and women separately, so they were not explained by differences between men and women.

## How big is the difference?

The d figures are a standard measure of how far apart two groups are, and 0.27 to 0.40 counts as modest. To put the largest one in everyday terms: pick one dog person and one cat person at random, and the dog person will be the more extraverted of the two about 61% of the time. If there were no difference at all, it would be 50%.

The score ranges of the two groups overlap by roughly 84% to 89% across the five traits. Plenty of cat people are outgoing and organised, and plenty of dog people are open and prone to worry. These two figures are worked out from the d values above; the paper reports the d values, not these translations of them.

## Why the difference?

The study cannot say. It shows that the two groups differ, not whether personality draws people to a kind of pet, whether living with a pet shapes personality, or whether something else, such as how people grew up, affects both. The authors suggest future work should look at childhood experience with pets and at what leads people to identify with one animal over another.

They also checked whether people were simply describing themselves flatteringly, since most traits have a more admired end. They thought this unlikely, because dog people scored lower on openness, which people tend to see as a good thing.

## What it does not mean

Your choice of pet does not reveal your personality, and these results do not say that either kind of person is better. Neuroticism here is an ordinary personality trait, the tendency to worry and feel stress, not a sign of anything wrong. More than 40% of people in the study called themselves both or neither, and the research says nothing about them.

## What it means for your results

If you are a cat person who scores high on extraversion, you are in good company. The five-minute Big Five test shows where you fall on all five traits against more than 600,000 people, which tells you far more than which animal you prefer.
