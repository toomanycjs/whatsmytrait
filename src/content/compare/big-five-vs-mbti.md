The Big Five, for almost every purpose. It measures five continuous traits with published reliability figures and a long record of predicting real outcomes, and its free versions are as good as the paid ones. The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator sorts people into sixteen types by splitting four continuous scales down the middle, which is why a large share of people who retake it get a different type, and why it is not used in personality research. The MBTI is better at one thing: giving people a memorable label and a vocabulary to talk about difference, which is a real use, just not a measurement.

## Side by side

| | Big Five | MBTI |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| What it measures | Five continuous traits, with facets | Four preferences, each split into two, giving sixteen types |
| How it is scored | Sum of items per scale, compared with a norm sample as a percentile | Each scale split at the midpoint into one of two letters |
| Reliability | Scale alphas typically .8 to .9; test-retest .7 to .9 over months | Scale scores are moderately reliable; the type is not, because people near a midpoint flip letters on retest |
| Predictive validity | Predicts work, health and relationship outcomes with small to moderate effects (Roberts and others, 2007) | Little evidence that type predicts outcomes beyond what its underlying continuous scores predict |
| Relation to each other | The four MBTI scales correlate with four of the Big Five: extraversion, openness, agreeableness and conscientiousness (McCrae and Costa, 1989); the MBTI has no neuroticism scale | |
| Who owns it | Public domain versions (IPIP) are free for any use | A trademark of the Myers-Briggs Company; the official test is paid and administered by certified practitioners |
| Where it is weak | Self-report, non-representative norm samples, and five broad traits miss much (see below) | Type instability, no neuroticism, and a theory of fixed categories the data do not support (Pittenger, 2005) |

## Where the Big Five is weak

Honesty requires saying so. The Big Five is a description, not an explanation; it tells you where you sit, not why. It measures self-image, which can be shaded or faked. Its norm samples, including the ones on this site, are people who took a test online, not a cross-section of any country. Five broad traits leave out honesty and humility, which the six-factor HEXACO model adds, and they say nothing about interests, values, abilities or what a person is like on a particular day. And a percentile against a sample of strangers is a comparison, not a judgement of worth.

## Which to take for which purpose

For understanding yourself with figures you can trust, for research, for comparing yourself with other people, or for seeing whether you have changed: the Big Five, and on this site the 120-item version with its facets. For a shared vocabulary in a team workshop, where the point is conversation rather than measurement: the MBTI does that job and the Big Five does not. For hiring: neither, and this site does not help with that.
